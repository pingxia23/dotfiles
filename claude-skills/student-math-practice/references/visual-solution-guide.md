# Clear worked solutions across math topics

The preferred solution is a guided explanation of what to do next and why. It connects the learner's attention to the relevant part of the problem instead of presenting a collection of calculations and leaving the learner to reconstruct the reasoning.

## Start from the concept

Choose a method the learner knows that teaches the requested idea. Do not introduce an advanced shortcut simply because it is quicker for the author. Use plain directions and define any new term at the point of use.

Each meaningful step should answer:

- What am I trying to find or show now?
- What information am I using?
- What action do I take, and why does it help?
- What do I now know?

Not every problem needs four written sentences. Match the explanation to the task: a direct calculation may need two lines; a reasoning problem may need several illustrated stages.

## Choose a representation that explains the idea

| Concept or reasoning need | Useful visual, when appropriate |
| --- | --- |
| Magnitude, order, distance, or change | Number line with labeled marks and movements |
| Fractions or proportional parts | Equal-length strips, area models, or double number lines |
| Place value or regrouping | Place-value chart or grouped quantities |
| Geometry or measurement | Labeled shape with the relevant lengths, angles, or regions highlighted |
| Data or relationships | Table, graph, or annotated chart |
| Equations or symbolic manipulation | Aligned equations with the changed term or operation marked |
| Word problems | Highlighted quantities, labeled bar model, or a small relationship diagram |
| Connected grid clues | Original grid with the current row, column, and target marked |

These are choices, not requirements. Use a visual only when it helps explain the mathematical relationship or removes a real source of confusion.

## Keep the visual and reasoning synchronized

- Put the relevant problem representation next to its explanation. Highlight the current part, not everything at once.
- Start from the given information. In each later state, carry forward only results established earlier or at that step.
- Keep labels, scale, orientation, and the meaning of colors stable unless changing them is the lesson. Explain an intentional change.
- Distinguish trials from established results with words as well as styling. Remove a rejected trial from later states. Do not invent a trial process for a direct problem.
- End with the completed diagram when the task involves filling, drawing, labeling, or constructing one. Check the result in context.
- Keep explanations valid without color. Use outlines, labels, arrows, or shading patterns alongside any colors.

## Example: comparing fractions with the same denominator

Suppose the learning goal is to explain why 3/8 is greater than 2/8, with equal denominators only and no decimal conversion. Those constraints apply to the explanation as well as the question.

1. **Identify the comparison.** Show two equal-length strips, each split into eight equal parts. Label one 3/8 and the other 2/8. Explain: “Both wholes are the same size, and both are split into eight equal parts.”
2. **Mark the amounts.** Shade three parts of the first strip and two of the second. Explain: “The pieces are the same size. Compare how many pieces are shaded.”
3. **Compare.** Highlight the one extra shaded part in the first strip. Explain: “Three eighths has one more eighth than two eighths, so 3/8 > 2/8.”
4. **Check.** Keep the completed strips beside the conclusion. Verify that the wholes and piece sizes match. Do not change to decimals or unlike denominators to check this answer.

This set could vary the practice through shading a named fraction, comparing two labeled models, placing fractions on an eighths number line, and explaining an incorrect comparison. The variety tests the same concept without adding excluded topics.

## Example: solving an equation without a separate picture

If the learner is practicing one-step equations, the expression itself may be the clearest representation:

> x + 5 = 12. We want x by itself. Subtract 5 from both sides to keep the equation balanced. Then x = 7. Check: 7 + 5 = 12.

Show aligned equations and mark the subtraction on both sides if useful. A decorative diagram or several repeated screenshots would not necessarily help. Do not use this method when algebra is outside the learner's allowed scope.
